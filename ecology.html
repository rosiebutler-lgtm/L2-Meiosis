Learning phase 1

Ecosystem conditions and organism distribution

ObjectiveExplain how biotic and abiotic factors influence the distribution and abundance of organisms.

Distribution vs abundance

Distribution is where organisms are found in a habitat.

Abundance is how many there are, as a count or a percentage cover.

Barnacles are found high on the shore (distribution) at about 40 per quadrat (abundance).

Abiotic factors

Non-living physical or chemical conditions.

  • Light intensity
  • Temperature
  • Water / soil moisture
  • Soil pH and nutrients
  • Wind exposure
  • Salinity and time out of water (rocky shore)

Biotic factors

Effects of other living things.

  • Predation (whelks eating mussels)
  • Competition for light, space, water or food
  • Grazing (sheep, possums)
  • Disease and parasites
  • Food supply and pollinators

Tolerance and limiting factors

Every species survives only within a range of tolerance for each condition. Outside it, it can't survive or reproduce.

The factor in shortest supply is the limiting factor. It sets how big a population can get.

Learning phase 2

Sampling abundance and distribution

ObjectiveUse quadrats and transects appropriately to collect ecological data and justify sampling choices.

Why sample?

You can't count every plant in a field, so you count in small, representative areas and scale up.

More samples give more reliable results, because one unusual quadrat has less effect on the mean.

Quadrats

A square frame of known area, often 0.5 m × 0.5 m = 0.25 m², or 1 m².

  • Count individuals you can tell apart (daisies, limpets).
  • Estimate percentage cover when you can't (grass, moss, lichen).

Random quadrats

Use these to compare abundance in two areas, e.g. mown lawn vs long grass.

Lay two tapes as axes, pick coordinates with a random number generator. Random placement avoids bias.

Transects

Use these where conditions change gradually across a habitat (a gradient), e.g. bush to paddock, low tide to high tide.

  • Line transect: record what touches the tape at regular intervals.
  • Belt transect: place quadrats at regular intervals along the tape.

Estimating a population

population ≈ mean per quadrat × (total area ÷ quadrat area)

Mean 6 daisies per 0.25 m² quadrat on a 100 m² lawn: 100 ÷ 0.25 = 400 quadrats, so 6 × 400 = 2400 daisies.

Measure where you sample

Take abiotic readings at each quadrat. Then every count of organisms has matching conditions, and you can look for a relationship.

Choose relevant variables

Pick variables that change across the habitat and could affect the organisms. On a rocky shore, time out of water matters more than soil pH.

Make readings reliable

Repeat readings and calculate a mean. Use the same equipment, method and time of day for every point.

Learning phase 4

Analysing ecological patterns

ObjectiveAnalyse ecological data to identify relationships and construct evidence-based explanations.

Mean

mean = total of all values ÷ number of values

Counts 4, 7, 5, 9, 5: total = 30, and there are 5 values. Mean = 30 ÷ 5 = 6.

Range

range = highest value − lowest value

Counts 4, 7, 5, 9, 5: range = 9 − 4 = 5. A big range means the results are spread out.

Anomalies

An anomaly is a result that doesn't fit the pattern of the others. Check it or repeat it. If you leave it out of the mean, say so and explain why.

Describing a pattern

Use “As ___ increases, ___ increases / decreases”. Quote data with units: “fern cover fell from 65% at 0 m to 0% at 20 m”.

A correlation doesn't prove one thing causes the other.

Claim, Evidence, Reasoning

  • Claim: what the pattern is.
  • Evidence: numbers from the table.
  • Reasoning: the biology that explains it.
